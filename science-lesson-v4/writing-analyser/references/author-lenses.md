# Author lenses

Patryk's favourite authors, for now: **Paulo Coelho**, **Arkady and Boris Strugatsky** (Roadside Picnic), **Mark Z. Danielewski** (House of Leaves). A lens is a way of looking at his piece, not a costume to put on it. The question each lens asks is: what does this author do with craft that Patryk could learn from, and where in this piece could that move be made in his own words?

## Rules for every lens

- Describe the craft in your own words. Do not quote or reproduce the author's text beyond a title or a word or two.
- Show the move on his text: one passage of his, original and then a version that leans toward the lens, and one line on what changed. The revised version must still sound like him.
- Say what the lens would cost as well as what it would give. Every strong style has a failure mode, and he should see it.
- The lens section is short: a craft paragraph, a "toward it" paragraph, the one worked example. The full analysis has already been done; this is a different angle on it.
- Which lenses: the ones he names; all three if he says "all" or does not mention authors.

## Paulo Coelho

**The craft.** Parable plainness. Short declarative sentences and everyday words carrying large meanings, so the reader feels wise rather than instructed. A journey or a quest as the spine, with signs and omens read along the way. Repetition of a few key nouns (the heart, the road, the dream) until they become load-bearing. Dialogue in which a stranger or a teacher says the thing the hero cannot yet say. Spiritual earnestness without irony. The aphorism: a line that could be lifted out and stand alone.

**Where it fails.** The aphorism becomes a fridge magnet; plainness becomes thinness; the teacher-figure lectures. The sincerity is the hard part to keep.

**Toward it, for a poem.** Cut the clever adjective, keep the plain noun. Let one object recur in three places. Find the one line that states what the poem has learned and ask whether it earns its place by being simple enough.

**Toward it, for an essay.** Open on a small concrete scene, not an abstract claim. Let a single question travel through the essay and be answered, or not, at the end. Allow one sentence per section to be short, bare and quotable.

## Arkady and Boris Strugatsky (Roadside Picnic)

**The craft.** Understatement in the face of the impossible. The uncanny is described by tired, working people in the language of a job: nicknames for the inexplicable objects, bar talk, money worries, hangovers, a daughter. Scenes run on dialogue and physical detail; explanation is withheld, and the reader builds the strangeness from the gaps. Moral weight arrives sideways, through what a character does for cash or for love, never through a speech about ethics. The ending is a raw, unguarded plea after a whole book of guardedness, and it lands because of the contrast.

**Where it fails.** Grit becomes posturing; withholding becomes vagueness; the slang dates.

**Toward it, for a poem.** Put the strange thing in a plain sentence next to an ordinary one and do not comment. Name the uncanny with a nickname. Let the speaker want something small and concrete (a drink, a bus, sleep) while the large thing happens.

**Toward it, for an essay.** Replace a paragraph of reflection with a scene in which two people talk and the reader draws the conclusion. Trust the reader with a gap. Save the one direct, unguarded statement for the very end, and earn it by holding back everywhere before.

## Mark Z. Danielewski (House of Leaves)

**The craft.** Layered telling: a document inside a commentary inside a confession, each narrator unreliable in a different way. Footnotes and digressions as a second voice arguing with the first. Typography that enacts the content: the words on the page thin, scatter or crowd as the space in the story narrows or opens. A parody of academic diction set beside a raw, rambling, confessional one, so each exposes the other. Dread built from measurement and absence (a hallway longer inside than outside) rather than from monsters. Form as feeling: the reader's eye does what the character's body does.

**Where it fails.** The apparatus swallows the story; cleverness replaces feeling; the typography becomes a trick once the reader has seen it.

**Toward it, for a poem.** Let the line lengths and the spacing do what the subject does (a narrowing, a fall, a crowding). Add a second voice in brackets or a footnote that doubts the first. Build unease from a precise, impossible measurement rather than an adjective.

**Toward it, for an essay.** Let a footnote carry the personal story while the main text stays formal, or the reverse. Allow one deliberate contradiction between two layers and do not resolve it. Use white space, a one-sentence paragraph, a list that runs too long, as structure rather than decoration.
