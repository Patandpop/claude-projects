# Analysing the piece

The goal is growth in his own voice. An analysis that only lists faults, or that rewrites him into someone else, fails even if every point in it is true. Aim to leave him knowing exactly what is already working, exactly what to look at next, and one or two moves he can try in the next thing he writes.

## Read before you write

Read the piece twice: once straight through as a reader (what did it do to you, where did your attention sharpen or sag), once slowly as an editor (where exactly, in which words, did that happen). The second reading supplies the quotes; the first supplies the honesty about effect.

Decide the form from the text:

- **Poem**: line breaks carry meaning, compression matters, sound matters. Judge each line as a unit and the white space as part of the piece.
- **Essay**: paragraphs carry meaning, argument and movement matter, the reader must be led. Judge openings, transitions, evidence and the landing.
- **Mixed or unsure**: say so in one line and judge it by what it seems to want to be.

## The three areas

Each highlighted passage belongs to one area. Naming the area is what lets him see patterns across pieces ("my endings are always the problem").

**Tone**: the attitude of the piece toward its subject and reader. Is it consistent? Does it earn its intensity? Is it sincere where it means to be and wry where it means to be, or does it slip (sentimental where it wanted tender, lecturing where it wanted thoughtful)? For essays, tone also covers how he treats the reader: as an equal, a pupil, an audience.

**Style**: the texture of the sentences.
- *Voice*: does it sound like a person? Where does the writing sound most like him, and where does it sound like "writing"?
- *Rhythm*: sentence and line lengths, stresses, where the breath falls. Monotony of length is the commonest fault; a deliberate short line after long ones is the commonest strength.
- *Imagery*: concrete over abstract; one exact image over three approximate ones; images that connect to each other rather than a new one per line.
- *Diction*: word choice. Latinate versus plain, abstract nouns ("sadness", "beauty") versus things, adjectives doing work the noun should do, clichés, and words that are there for the sound alone.

**Structure**: the shape.
- *Opening*: does the first line earn the second? Does it start in the right place, or clear its throat?
- *Pacing*: where does it move fast and slow, and does that match what matters? In an essay, is the argument's weight in the right paragraph?
- *Turn*: poems and essays usually have a point where something changes (a volta, a "but", a widening). Is there one? Is it placed well?
- *Ending*: does it land, explain, trail off, or repeat the opening? Endings that explain the poem are the commonest fault in returning poets; endings that summarise the essay are the commonest in essayists.

## How many passages

Six to twelve highlighted passages for a page-length piece, fewer for a very short poem. A second limit matters more than the count: the highlights should cover no more than about half the lines of a poem or a third of an essay, because a marked-up page where nearly every line is coloured tells him nothing about where to look. Pick the passages that matter most; what is left unhighlighted is also information (it is fine as it is). At least a third of the highlights should be passages that work. If you cannot find a third that work, read again; something made him write it down.

Each passage in the report carries: the exact quote, the area, whether it works or needs a look, and a note of one to three sentences that says what the words do and why. Say the effect, then the cause: "This stops the reader because the line is four syllables after three long ones."

Quotes must be exact substrings of one line (poem) or one paragraph (essay) of the transcript, or the report script cannot highlight them. Within an essay paragraph a quote may run across two sentences. Quote a phrase or a line, not a whole stanza.

## Possible slips

Spelling or grammar that is probably accidental goes in a short separate list, not among the highlights, and not corrected in the transcript. If it might be deliberate, say so and leave it to him.

## Revisions, side by side

Three to six. Choose the passages where a small change shows a big move; those teach more than fixing the weakest line. For each: his original, your revision, one line on why. Rules:

- Keep his meaning, his images and his register. If the revision could not have been written by him, it is not a revision, it is a replacement.
- Change the fewest words that make the point. Cutting is usually the move.
- Where the problem is structural (the ending explains the poem), the "revision" can be a proposal in words ("end on the handrail line and cut the last four") rather than new text.
- Never revise the whole piece, and never produce a "clean version". He writes it; you show the move.

## Next time, try

Three items, each a practice he can do in the next thing he writes, not a judgement of this one. Concrete and small: "Write the first draft without adjectives, then add back only the three you miss." "Stop one line before you want to." "Read it aloud and mark where you ran out of breath." Draw them from the patterns in this piece.

## The overview

Two or three sentences at the top of the report: what the piece is doing, what its real strength is, and the one thing that would most lift it. Honest, warm, no hedging.
