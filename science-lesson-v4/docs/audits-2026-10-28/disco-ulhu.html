Disco Elysium for Ulhu

What ZA/UM's 2019 detective game does with words, paint and failure, and which of those ideas would make Ulhu's Rituals feel deeper without making it nag.

Vespers, 21:12. The book is open.

What the game actually does

Disco Elysium is a role-playing game with no combat. You are a detective who wakes up with no memory, and almost everything happens in conversation. Nine things it does well matter for Ulhu.

The writing: second person

The narrator talks to you as "you", and describes your body, your hangover and your shame as if they were scenery. The tone swings between slapstick and grief in a single paragraph. The joke lands first and the sadness arrives a beat later, so neither feels cheap.

Lesson: a line can be funny and true at once. Ulhu's current lines are funny; the next step is letting some of them also be true.

24 skills as inner voices

Your skills are not numbers. They are characters inside your head, in four houses (Intellect, Psyche, Physique, Motorics). Logic is a pedant, Electrochemistry wants you to drink the spilled beer, Inland Empire talks to your necktie. Kurvitz said the skills have egos and want you to listen to them. The more points a skill has, the more often it speaks.

Lesson: the parts of a person can argue. A voice gets louder as you feed it.

Passive checks

Most checks happen silently. If your skill is high enough, a voice just chimes in with something useful or strange, and the line is tagged with its name and difficulty, for example [Logic: Easy, success]. You never asked for it. You simply notice more because of who you have become.

Lesson: reward that arrives unasked, as a comment, without a button to press.

The Thought Cabinet

Odd ideas you stumble on become "thoughts". You choose whether to internalise one. It then sits in a slot and takes hours or days of in-game time to work through. While it does, it often carries a small penalty. When it finishes, you get the solution text (Kurvitz: Each is a riddle, posing a question for you to answer) and a lasting effect. Slots are limited and you can forget a thought for a cost.

Lesson: change is slow, chosen and visible. You watch yourself become something.

Failure as content

Failing a check never just says "failed". Something happens: you trip, say the wrong thing, get laughed at, or learn something sideways. Some failures help you. One review put it simply: It's written to embrace failure. Players who reload to avoid failing miss some of the best scenes.

Lesson: a slip should open a scene, not close a door.

White and red checks

Active checks show your odds as a percentage before you try. White checks can be retried once you have improved the skill. Red checks happen once, and you live with the result either way. The colour tells you how much is at stake before you commit.

Lesson: show the stakes honestly up front. But note the frustration critics raised below.

The dialogue column

All talk scrolls in one tall column on the right of the screen, like a script. Speakers' names are in capitals, coloured by their house. Choices are numbered, plain choices in orange, skill-unlocked choices in brackets. The world stays visible on the left.

Lesson: one calm place for words, so the rest of the screen can stay quiet.

The art

Aleksander Rostov led the art: loose oil-paint brushwork, rough expressionist portraits with smeared faces and harsh colour accents, painters like Repin and Vrubel named as influences. Muted ochres, rust and sea-greys with one loud colour per portrait. The team wanted to balance the mundane with the unfamiliar and strange.

Lesson: portraits carry character. Ugliness painted with care reads as love.

Identity, politics, sound and small things

The game quietly counts how you talk. After about four leanings, a voice offers to make it official, and you can say yes or no. Separate "cop types" (Sorry Cop, Superstar Cop, Apocalypse Cop) are earned from your habits of speech. British Sea Power's BAFTA-winning score sits low and grey under everything. And small actions matter: a mailbox, a necktie, a broken lamp can each start a conversation.

Lesson: the game notices who you are being, names it, and lets you accept or refuse the name.

What critics and designers praised

  • The skills as voices: reviewers called them the game's best idea, because they turn a stat sheet into a personality.
  • Failure that is fun to read, so players stop reloading.
  • The Thought Cabinet as "a fascinating concept" that works in practice (RPGFan).
  • The soundtrack (BAFTA for music) and the painted look, which made a small budget feel like an art gallery.
  • Writing that made a broken man funny without making fun of his pain.

What they doubted

  • Too many words: one essay estimated a script of around 2,000 pages, and some reviewers called the prose purple.
  • Kurvitz himself called the Thought Cabinet a "quagmire" that nearly sank the project.
  • Players hated passing five checks and failing the last one, and progress gated behind a single roll.
  • Some thoughts were "best" for their numbers, so players picked them for the stats and not the meaning.

The one thing to hold on to

Disco Elysium is played for hours in long reading sessions. Ulhu is used in ten-second taps between lessons. So the words belong in the evening. Fast by day, wordy by night: the day screens stay as tap-quick as they are now, and Vespers and High Vespers become the place where the voices, the thoughts and the painting live.

Ideas for Ulhu, judged

Each idea has how it would feel, the effort, and a check against your four rules: no nagging, nothing that decides for you, no automatic work after 7 pm, and honesty about bad habits rewarded. Green fits, amber needs care, red clashes.

Skills as inner voices

Your identities speak

The 16 identities (Diligent, Wordsmith, Leviathan, Homeflame and the rest) become voices, grouped into four houses by colour: Teaching, Body, Mind, People. Each voice has a personality. Diligent is dry and proud, Leviathan is physical and blunt, Serene is slow and a bit spooky, Frugal is a miser who loves you. A voice only speaks when you have just tapped something it cares about, and it gets more to say as its identity collects votes.

Feels like: you tick "Gym" and a gruff voice says one sentence about your shoulders. Over a term you notice which voices have grown loud. That tells you who you are becoming without a single chart.

Effort Medium
No nagFits if voices only answer your taps and never say "you should".
Your callFits. They comment, they never choose.
After 7Fits. No timed messages at all.
HonestyFits. Curses get voices too, and they are on your side.

Do it. One line at most, shown where Ulhu's speech already sits, so no new clutter.

Passive checks

Checks that pass by themselves

Instead of generic praise at 7, 30 and 66 nights, a tagged line appears: [Diligent: Medium, success]. The "difficulty" comes from your own record (a ritual you kept 9 Tuesdays in a row is Easy; your first clean Friday in a month is Hard). Success lines celebrate. The rare "failure" lines are funny, never shaming.

Feels like: the game noticed something true about you before you did.

Effort Small
No nagFits. It fires on your own tap or in Vespers.
Your callFits.
After 7Fits.
HonestyFits. A curse log can pass a check too ("[Scholar] You logged it at 21:40, which means you were watching yourself").

Do it with the voices. The cheapest way to make streak moments feel personal.

The Thought Cabinet

"Who you are becoming" as thoughts you internalise

In High Vespers, when an identity crosses a vote mark, a sealed thought appears, named like a riddle: The Tide That Never Empties (Diligent), The Crisp Hour (from honest Flesh logs). You may take it into one of three slots, or leave it. It "cooks" for a week. While cooking it shows its question. The next High Vespers it opens with its answer and a small lasting effect: a slightly richer multiplier on that identity's rituals, a new Ulhu outfit, or a Sanctum object. Forgetting one costs runes.

Feels like: a cabinet of curiosities about yourself, slowly filling. The reward is mostly the words, so it cannot be gamed the way critics complained about.

Effort Large
No nagFits. Offered once a week, inside a screen you chose to open.
Your callFits strongly. You decide what to internalise.
After 7Fits. Nothing runs on its own.
HonestyFits strongly. Some thoughts can only grow from logged curses.

Do it after Vespers is built. Keep effects tiny so the meaning stays the point.

The dialogue column

Vespers as a conversation

The E7 book you picked opens, and inside is one tall column of text in second person. Ulhu narrates the day. The sigils (Flesh, Domain, Scene, Coven) speak as voices about each logged curse. Detail chips appear as numbered orange choices. The last option is always "Close the book".

Feels like: two minutes of being talked through your own day by a dark, funny friend, instead of filling in a form.

Effort Medium
No nagFits. It never opens itself.
Your callFits. Every choice is optional.
After 7Care. It lives in the evening, so it must stay short (under two minutes) and never turn into work.
HonestyFits. Logging is the conversation.

Do it. It matches your book and night-line picks and gives the voices a home.

Failure as content

Slips written as scenes, not verdicts

Rewrite the 30 curse lines in the Disco shape: a short scene in second person, then a sideways insight. "The phone was in your hand before you decided anything. Your thumb has a better memory than you." Absolution becomes "failing forward": the slip opened the door to the antidote, and the glass cracking is the scene that follows.

Feels like: logging a curse gives you a small, good piece of writing. Being honest is the way to read more of the book.

Effort Small
No nagFits. Lines only answer a log.
Your callFits.
After 7Fits.
HonestyFits strongly, as long as no line moralises or says "you should have".

Do it first. Words only, no new screens.

Copotypes

Earned titles you can refuse

High Vespers notices patterns and offers a title for the term: "The Lantern Teacher" (lessons built a week ahead), "The Apologetic Cultist" (many Absolutions won), "The Midnight Marker" (curse logs after 22:00 on marking nights). A voice offers it. You can accept it, and it shows under Ulhu's level, or decline it with a single tap.

Feels like: being seen, with a wink. Some titles are flattering, some are rude, all are fair.

Effort Small
No nagFits. Weekly, inside High Vespers.
Your callFits. Accept or decline, like the game.
After 7Fits.
HonestyCare. Rude titles must come from logged curses lightly, so logging never feels like it earns a label you hate.

Good, cheap fun. Ask you to approve the title list first.

White and red checks

Quest steps marked by stakes

White steps can be missed and come back (most quests already work like that). Red steps would be one-shot pacts: a boss deadline you swear to, sealed with the F9 wax, with no second try. The game's odds percentage could come from your own history ("you keep 18 of 25 Friday promises").

Feels like: drama when it is a pact you chose. Pressure when it is a deadline that chose you.

Effort Medium
No nagCare. A percentage can read as a judgement before you start.
Your callCare. Only red if you mark it red yourself.
After 7Fits.
HonestyClash. One-shot loss fights "never miss twice" kindness and the critics' main complaint.

Take only the white idea, as wording ("it returns"), plus an optional red "pact" for bosses. Skip the odds.

The art

Painted portraits

Your first brief asked for the painterly Disco look, and your app icon already uses it. But the decided looks are pixel Ulhu, stone, glass, and an HD-2D stall with a 16-bit fishmonger. Painting everything would fight those picks. The place it fits is the column: small painted busts beside the speaker names in Vespers (Ulhu, the four sigils), and painted covers on sealed thoughts.

Feels like: at night the app changes medium, from pixels to oil, as if you stepped into a gallery.

Effort Large
No nagFits.
Your callFits.
After 7Fits.
HonestyFits.

Mock up five busts first. Drawing in code limits how painterly they can be, so judge by eye before committing.

Feels like: a scene with two characters and you in the middle, not a menu with a discount.

Top 5 recommendations

In the order I would build them. All five fit your four rules.

  1. Rewrite slips as scenes

    Turn the 30 curse lines into short second-person scenes with a sideways insight, and frame Absolution as failing forward. Words only, small effort, and it makes honesty the way to read more of the story.

  2. Make Vespers a dialogue column

    The E7 book opens into one column of text: Ulhu narrates, the four sigils speak about each logged curse, detail chips are numbered choices, and "Close the book" is always there. Medium effort, and it gives every other idea a home. Keep it under two minutes.

  3. Let the identities speak as inner voices

    Group the 16 identities into four coloured houses with their own personalities. A voice speaks one line only when you tap something it cares about, and grows louder as its identity collects votes. Never an instruction, never a timed message.

  4. Use passive checks for streak moments

    Replace generic streak praise with tagged lines like "[Diligent: Medium, success]", with difficulty taken from your own record. Small effort, and it makes the 7, 30 and 66 night moments feel personal.

  5. Add a Thought Cabinet to High Vespers

    When an identity crosses a vote mark, offer a sealed thought you can choose to internalise in one of three slots. It cooks for a week and opens with its answer and a tiny effect. Some thoughts grow only from honestly logged curses. Large effort, so build it after Vespers works.

Hold back on: red one-shot checks and odds percentages (they clash with never-miss-twice), painting everything (it fights your pixel, stone and glass picks; try busts in the column first), and sound (off by default if ever).
