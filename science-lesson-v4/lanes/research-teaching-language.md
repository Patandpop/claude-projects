# What language works on teaching slides and in spoken prompts: research and proposed rules (30 Oct 2026)

Patryk asked (14 Oct) for deep research on the best language for teaching before any new wording rules, after reading that a plain concise natural voice beats rigid controlled English (ASD-STE100), that visuals and interactive tools beat text simplification, and that reading ease should suit the audience. He also asked (13 and 19 Oct) for Engage hooks that are real questions, Learn slides with less text and better key-word highlighting, and MCQ wrong options that are each one logical mistake away. Free sources only. Written in the Lane C design chat; nothing here changes the skill until he says yes, and the wording then goes into SKILL.md through Lane B.

## 1. What the evidence says, in plain terms

### 1.1 Slides: fewer words, placed next to the picture they explain (Mayer)
Richard Mayer's multimedia principles are the best-tested guidance for slides. The ones that matter here:
- **Coherence:** cut anything that is not needed for the point. Decoration and extra sentences cost learning.
- **Signalling:** cues that show what matters (bold, underline, an arrow) help; they only work if used sparingly.
- **Redundancy:** text that repeats what the teacher is saying, word for word, hurts. Short on-screen text that names the key action, placed next to the part of the picture it is about, is the exception that works.
- **Contiguity:** words and the picture they explain sit together on the screen, not apart.
- **Segmenting:** one chunk at a time, learner paced (a click per step), beats one dense slide.
- **Pre-training:** teach the names of the parts before the explanation that uses them (this is the objectives slide's "words for this lesson").
- **Personalisation:** conversational wording ("you", "we") beats formal wording.
- Boundary: experts need less of this (the expertise reversal effect), so A-level slides can carry more than Year 7 ones.
Sources: [Pitt multimedia principles checklist](https://teaching.pitt.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/multimedia-principles-checklist.pdf), [NUS summary](https://medicine.nus.edu.sg/medixperience/?p=148), [Dartmouth summary](https://services.dartmouth.edu/TDClient/1806/Portal/KB/PrintArticle?ID=171655). Primary: Mayer, *Multimedia Learning* (2009) and Mayer (2008) *American Psychologist*.

### 1.2 Dual coding works only when the words and the picture are about the same thing at the same time
The Learning Scientists back dual coding (words plus a matching diagram) and separate it from the debunked learning-styles idea. David Didau's criticism is the useful limit: it helps only when the two are "conceptually coherent, temporally aligned and cognitively economical"; "add pictures" on its own can split attention. Durrington Research School suggests flow diagrams for processes and drawing the summary diagram while teaching it, and warns that photos and videos can distract. The RSC says the same: attractive is not the same as dual coded.
Sources: [Learning Scientists](https://learningscientists.org/blog/2019/6/6-1), [Didau, The dual coding delusion](https://daviddidau.substack.com/p/the-dual-coding-delusion), [Durrington research bites](https://researchschool.org.uk/durrington/news/research-bites-dual-coding), [RSC live drawing](https://edu.rsc.org/ideas/how-to-harness-dual-coding/4010629.article).
What this means for us: the Learn dual-coding mock-ups (lanes/mockups/learn-dual-coding.html) are the right direction only if each note sits by its part and nothing else is on the slide.

### 1.3 Vocabulary: teach the technical words, and the "academic" words that hide in exam questions
The EEF's secondary science guidance makes explicit vocabulary teaching one of its seven recommendations. Tier 3 words (the science terms) are already handled; the gap for weaker readers is tier 2 (academic words used across subjects: "factor", "rate", "estimate", "justify"). Pupils need to know about 95% of the words in a text to read it; a few tier 2 words block a question more often than the science does. Some everyday words carry a different science meaning (force, material, energy) and need saying out loud.
Sources: [Durrington on the EEF literacy report](https://researchschool.org.uk/durrington/news/the-new-guidance-report-from-the-eef-improving-literacy-in-secondary-schools), [RSC, keys to language](https://edu.rsc.org/feature/handing-out-the-keys-to-language-learning/4015069.article), [National Literacy Trust, literacy in science](https://cdn.literacytrust.org.uk/media/documents/Developing_literacy_in_science_for_secondary_schools.pdf), [Chartered College on disciplinary vocabulary](https://my.chartered.college/?p=18814).

### 1.4 Readability numbers: a guide, not a rule
Nobody has published Flesch scores for GCSE science papers. Exam boards do the work by hand: Pearson's reviews use language modifiers who check sentence structure, word choice and syllable count; AQA's "Making questions clear" booklet and its exams explainer say the aim is to assess the science, not comprehension, and that demand comes from concept, context and command words together. Public-sector plain-English guidance converges on an average of 15 to 20 words a sentence (GOV.UK's older limit was 25), with one cited finding that at an average of 14 words readers understand over 90% of a text. Flesch counts syllables and sentence length, so "photosynthesis" and "electromagnetic" push a perfectly clear sentence into "difficult": the score is a check on sentence length, not a target to chase.
Sources: [Pearson, accessible exam papers](https://qualifications.pearson.com/content/dam/pdf/GCSE/Science/2016/teaching-and-learning-materials/Our_focus_on_accessible_exam_papers.pdf), [AQA GCSE science exams explained](https://filestore.aqa.org.uk/resources/science/AQA-GCSE-SCIENCE-EXAMS-EXPLAINED.PDF), [GOV.UK sentence length](https://insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk/2014/08/04/sentence-length-why-25-words-is-our-limit/), [Somerset, reading age](https://playbook.somerset.gov.uk/digital-inclusion-and-accessibility/creating-accessible-documents/document-language-and-reading-age), [NCSE readability](https://ncse.ie/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/5.3_Readability.pdf) (Flesch-Kincaid grade + 5 = reading age).

### 1.5 Command words are a fixed vocabulary; use the board's own meanings
AQA publishes its command words with one-line meanings (calculate, compare, describe, explain, evaluate, give, justify, suggest, use, and so on). Pupils must meet them in the lesson in the exact sense the exam uses them: "describe" is recall in order; "explain" is reasons; "compare" needs both things; "suggest" is applying knowledge to a new situation; "use" means the answer must come from the information given.
Source: [AQA command words](https://www.aqa.org.uk/resources/science/gcse/teach/command-words).

### 1.6 Instructions: the fewest words, as a statement, one step at a time
Lemov's "economy of language" and "what to do" (Teach Like a Champion) are practitioner wisdom, not controlled research, but they fit what is known about working memory: short directions ("Pens down", "Books open at the graph") beat polite long ones, statements beat questions ("Have a seat", not "Can you sit down?"), and a learner who struggles with language needs the same content in steps or in writing. Rosenshine's principles (from cognitive science and from studying effective teachers) say the same about content: small steps, many questions, check every pupil's answer.
Sources: [Difflearn, economy of language](https://blog.difflearn.com/2018/01/04/using-economy-language-teaching-2), [Structural Learning, strong voice](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/strong-voice), [K-State handout](https://www.k-state.edu/tlc/faculty_gta_development/professionaldevelopmentseries/past_pd_events/supplementary-information/Gaea%20Hock%20April%2023%202025.pdf), [Teacher Toolkit on Rosenshine](https://www.teachertoolkit.co.uk/2018/10/21/barack-rosenshine/), [InnerDrive guide](https://www.innerdrive.co.uk/blog/guide-rosenshine-10-principles/).

### 1.7 Hooks: a question with a gap the pupils can nearly close, resolved in the lesson
Loewenstein's information-gap theory (1994) is the mechanism behind a good Engage: curiosity comes from noticing a gap in what you know; the gap must feel closeable; you need some prior knowledge to feel it; and interest peaks when curiosity is "slightly piqued" (Kang et al., 2009, with better recall a week or two later for answers people were curious about). Willingham's line: teachers are "so eager to get to the answer that we do not devote sufficient time to developing the question". The one classroom study found (Kang and Kim, 2024, 700 pupils aged 10 to 12) says in-the-moment curiosity was the strongest driver of in-class learning, while longer-term interest in science depended on interest in the phenomenon itself. Nobody has tested named hook recipes in classrooms; the lab evidence supports the mechanism, not any particular format.
Sources: [Kang et al., The wick in the candle of learning](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf), [NOVA, how to stimulate curiosity](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/how-to-stimulate-curiosity/), [Thoughtful Classroom, curiosity catalysts](https://thoughtfulclassroom.com/2019/11/27/curiosity-catalysts-or-how-to-create-lesson-hooks-that-really-work), [NARST on Kang and Kim](https://narst.org/research-worth-reading/exploring-predictiveness-and-curiosity-and-interest-in-science-learning), [Psychology in Action review](https://www.psychologyinaction.org/?p=1225).

### 1.8 Multiple choice: each wrong option is a known mistake, and the right answer cannot be reached by a wrong route
Dylan Wiliam's hinge questions (Embedded Formative Assessment, 2011; Educational Leadership, 2015): every distractor matches a documented misconception (his example: the option you get by forgetting to halve the area, the option you get by squaring the diameter); the right answer must not be reachable by flawed reasoning; three or four options; quick to answer; what matters is the pattern of wrong answers, because one popular wrong option tells you what to re-teach.
Sources: [Fletcher-Wood slides via Wouldham](https://www.wouldham.kent.sch.uk/content/pages/documents/1603276777.pdf), [Teach First ECF module 4](https://manage-training-for-early-career-teachers.education.gov.uk/cip-materials/teach-first-ECF-Module-4-ECT-training-basic-programme-session-plan.pdf).
This is already the planner's "why" tag on each wrong option (the ABCD votes report the misconception). The rule below only writes it down for the lesson author.

- Engage: a real question the lesson answers, six hook mechanisms with no repeat in consecutive lessons, leave Engage out if no good hook fits (engage-library.md; lesson-quality final decisions, 19 Oct).

## 3. Proposed rules (for Patryk's yes; then Lane B puts them in SKILL.md)

### 3.1 Slides (any slide pupils read)
1. **One slide, one idea.** If a second idea needs its own sentence, it gets its own slide. (Mayer coherence and segmenting; Patryk's "no content split across two slides" means one idea is never split, not that two ideas share one slide.)
2. **Words sit by their picture.** A note about a part goes next to that part, not in a block on the other side. A slide with a diagram carries at most three notes of one sentence each.
3. **Sentences of 15 words or fewer on slides, 20 in notes.** Long technical words are fine; long sentences are not. The build warns at 20 words on a slide.
4. **The board never repeats the teacher word for word.** The slide carries the short version (the key action, the key term, the number); the full explanation is in the speaker notes, in Patryk's voice, for him to say.
5. **Reveal in steps.** Anything with parts (a process, a diagram, a worked example) appears one part per click with its one sentence. This is why the GUESS and build-it-up mock-ups reveal step by step.
6. **Say "you" and "we".** "Work out the pressure" and "We measure the length", not "The pressure is to be calculated" and "The length is measured by the student".
7. **A-level slides may carry more** (longer sentences, denser diagrams), because the expertise reversal effect means scaffolds that help Year 8 slow Year 13 down. The limits above apply to Years 7 to 11; Years 12 and 13 get 20 words a sentence and up to five notes.

### 3.2 Vocabulary
8. **Command words in the exam's sense, every time.** A question on a slide or sheet uses an AQA (or OCR) command word with its published meaning, and the first time a lesson uses a command word the objectives slide's "Words you know" band may define it ("Compare: say what is the same and what is different about both").
9. **One tier 2 word per lesson, taught on purpose.** Besides the science terms, the lesson names one academic word its questions depend on (factor, rate, trend, justify, estimate) and defines it where it first appears. The exam library can say which tier 2 words the real questions on this topic use.
10. **Everyday words with a science meaning get a line.** When a lesson uses force, energy, material, pressure, power, reaction or work, the Learn slide says the science meaning in one line and the speaker notes say the everyday one.

### 3.3 Spoken prompts and instructions (the lines Patryk says, and the "This lesson" strip)
11. **Instructions are statements, the fewest words that work, one action each.** "Books open. Copy the title." Not "Could everyone please make sure you have your books open and are ready to copy the title?" Under eight words a line; two actions means two lines.
12. **What to do, then why, never the other way round.** "Whiteboards up. I want to see every answer." The reason comes after the action.
13. **Instructions that pupils must follow alone also appear in writing** (the worksheet, the task slide), in the same words.

### 3.4 Engage hooks
14. **A hook is a question with a gap pupils can nearly close.** It must need something they already know (so they feel the gap) and one thing this lesson teaches (so the gap closes today). "Why does the sea stay cool when the sand burns your feet?" works; "What is specific heat capacity?" does not (no gap they can feel), and "Is your hair a virus?" does not (no real gap).
15. **Spend the time on the question, not the answer.** The Engage slide poses it and takes a commitment (a prediction, a hand up, a whiteboard choice); it never answers. The answer comes back on the Review slide with the same words, so the loop closes.
16. **The question is stated in under 15 words, in plain words, with no hype.** No "explore", "discover", "journey", "fascinating". If it needs a picture or a number to feel real, it gets one; if no honest question exists for the lesson, Engage is left out (already the rule).
17. **The hook stays live.** Speaker notes carry one line on where the lesson answers it, so Patryk can point back to it mid-lesson.

### 3.5 Multiple choice (hinge questions)
18. **Each wrong option is one known mistake,** named in the "why" tag (already required). A wrong option that no pupil would pick is replaced.
19. **The right answer cannot be reached by a wrong route.** If a common misconception also gives the right letter, the question is rewritten.
20. **Three or four options, one line each, answerable in under a minute,** so the ABCD vote keeps its pace.

## 4. How to check the rules once built (for Lane B)
- The build's existing word-count and split checks gain: slide sentence length (warn at 20 words for Years 7 to 11), notes present on every Learn slide, at most three notes on a diagram slide, an Engage question under 15 words with no hype word, every MCQ wrong option tagged.
- A fixture lesson that breaks each rule once, so each warning is seen to fire.
- Nothing is rewritten by the build; it warns and Claude fixes the content.

## 5. What this does not settle
- Readability numbers for Patryk's own classes: Flesch is a sentence-length check, not a target. If he wants a number, the honest one is average sentence length (under 15 on slides), which the build can report per lesson.
- Named hook formats: the six mechanisms stay because they match the mechanism (a closeable gap), not because any format has been tested in classrooms.
- Paid tools: none needed; nothing here calls an API.
