The pattern: two text boxes on the left, a picture on the right that nothing points at. The words carry the explanation and the picture decorates it. In the versions below the picture carries the explanation and the words label it.

What the research found

  • AI-sounding text comes from patterns: "crucial", "delve", "plays a key role", claims that something is important, "not just X, it's Y", lists of three, "scientists believe". The fix: name the thing, say what it does, cut the importance claim.
  • Sentence length: at most 15 words for KS3, 20 for KS4 and 25 for KS5. One name per idea; no swapping synonyms.
  • Less text: you are the narration, so the slide shouldn't carry the script. Use one full-sentence headline plus a picture as the evidence, with about 40 words of text at most.
  • Definitions use only everyday words. The most useful extra is a "Not:" line naming the common mistake (see F2).
  • Pictures: labels go on the picture, not in a key beside it. No decorative pictures. Use a diagram for "how it works" and a photo for "recognise the real thing".
  • Hooks that work: a gap pupils can almost close, a prediction, or questions asked before teaching. A surprise only helps if the lesson explains it later.
  • Hooks that fail: fun facts the lesson never uses ("seductive details" lower recall), and big demos with no prediction. When no good hook fits, skip Engage.

What the research says: bold works best, at most 3 terms per slide, and only the first time a term appears. Underlining is a poor choice because it reads as a link. Colour helps only when the same colour marks the part of the picture the word names (as in E1 and D2). So style 1, with colour kept for matching words to pictures, is the evidence-based pick.
The rule I would pair with any of these: only the key term is marked, the first time it appears on a slide, and at most three per slide. If everything is highlighted, nothing is.

10. Engage hooks

Each hook is a real question the lesson answers later. A draft menu; the research report will rank these.

Hooks to avoid (draft)

  • "Have you ever wondered…?" questions with no real puzzle in them.
  • Fun facts the lesson never comes back to. Research on "seductive details" shows they lower recall.
  • A big demo with no prediction. Pupils remember the bang, not the science.
  • "Imagine a world without…" openers. Vague, and the class knows it's filler.
  • A question the class can answer in one word without thinking.
